. AI Applications in U.S. Higher Education Institutions
3.1 Student Enrollment and Recruitment
AI technologies have become increasingly central to enrollment management strategies at U.S. colleges and universities. Institutions face intense competition for students, and AI-powered tools offer the promise of more efficient and effective recruitment processes. One prominent application involves using machine learning algorithms to predict enrollment yield—the likelihood that an admitted student will actually enroll. These predictive models analyze historical data on student characteristics, financial aid packages, engagement behaviors, and other variables to estimate enrollment probability (Aulck et al., 2020).
At a large public U.S. university, researchers developed a system that combines machine learning classifiers with genetic algorithms to optimize scholarship allocation. The goal was to maximize enrollment yield and tuition revenue while working within budget constraints. The system analyzes data from the Free Application for Federal Student Aid (FAFSA) along with institutional records to identify which students are most likely to enroll and which scholarship amounts would be most effective in influencing their decisions. While the specific institution was not named in the published research, the approach demonstrates how AI can inform strategic enrollment decisions (Aulck et al., 2020).
Another application of AI in enrollment involves bias analysis of admissions models. At a large urban research university, researchers examined AI models used for undergraduate admissions to the School of Science, specifically analyzing how these models performed across different demographic groups. The study evaluated predictive models for bias with respect to gender, race, and first-generation student status, incorporating fairness metrics to assess whether the AI systems were perpetuating or mitigating existing inequities in admissions processes (Busum et al., 2024).
The University of Texas at Austin’s Department of Computer Science developed GRADE, an explainable AI system that uses logistic regression on past admission records to estimate applicant admission probability. This system was designed to reduce the number of full application reviews required and decrease review time for admissions experts, while maintaining transparency about how admission decisions are made (Mallik et al., 2023).
3.2 Student Onboarding and Orientation
The transition from high school to college represents a critical juncture where many students encounter obstacles that can derail their educational plans. The phenomenon of “summer melt”—when students who have been admitted and committed to a college fail to enroll in the fall—has been a persistent challenge for U.S. institutions. AI-powered interventions have shown promise in addressing this issue.
Georgia State University (GSU) implemented a groundbreaking conversational AI system to support students during the summer between high school and college. The system, which uses text-message-based communication, provides personalized outreach and guidance for each pre-enrollment task that students must complete, such as submitting immunization records, completing financial aid paperwork, registering for orientation, and paying deposits. The AI system employs deep reinforcement learning and convolutional neural networks—technologies similar to those used in autonomous vehicles—to learn from student interactions and continuously improve its responses (Page et al., 2017).
A randomized field experiment at GSU demonstrated the effectiveness of this approach. Students assigned to receive AI-powered support exhibited greater success with pre-enrollment requirements and were 3.3 percentage points more likely to enroll on-time compared to the control group. Importantly, these enrollment impacts were comparable to those achieved through more labor-intensive human interventions, but with substantially reduced burden on university staff. The AI system could efficiently support thousands of students simultaneously, providing personalized guidance at scale (Page et al., 2017; Riep et al., 2024).
The GSU implementation represents a prescriptive AI system—one that actively guides students toward specific actions and provides proactive support. The system does not make decisions on behalf of students but rather facilitates their navigation of complex administrative processes. This approach has been particularly valuable for first-generation college students and those from underrepresented backgrounds, who may lack family members with college experience to guide them through these processes (Riep et al., 2024).
3.3 Academic Advising and Student Support
Academic advising represents another domain where AI technologies are being deployed across U.S. higher education institutions. Traditional advising models often struggle to provide timely, personalized support to all students, particularly at large institutions where advisor-to-student ratios can exceed 1:500. AI-powered advising systems aim to augment human advisors by providing data-driven insights, identifying at-risk students, and offering 24/7 access to basic advising information.
The University of Maryland’s A. James Clark School of Engineering developed a bias-aware AI chatbot specifically designed for engineering advising. This system was created to complement traditional advising services and address inequities experienced by underrepresented and first-generation students. The chatbot can answer common questions about course requirements, degree planning, and academic policies, freeing human advisors to focus on more complex student needs. Importantly, the system was designed with explicit attention to bias mitigation, recognizing that AI systems can inadvertently perpetuate existing inequities if not carefully developed (Kartholy et al., 2025).
Predictive analytics systems have been widely adopted for academic advising purposes. These systems integrate with student information systems like Banner to analyze student data and identify those at risk of academic difficulty or attrition. At U.S. universities, AI systems for academic advising typically analyze factors such as course grades, attendance patterns, engagement with learning management systems, demographic characteristics, and prior academic preparation. The systems generate risk scores or alerts that prompt advisors to reach out to students proactively (Renick, n.d.).
A study of predictive analytics-informed academic advising among undeclared first-year engineering students examined the impact of data analytics on STEM student success. The research found that when advisors used predictive analytics tools to identify and support at-risk students, there were measurable improvements in retention and academic performance. However, the study also highlighted the importance of human judgment in interpreting AI-generated insights and the need for advisors to be trained in using these tools effectively (Chen et al., 2020).
Elon University has implemented AI systems for tracking courses and course planning, helping students navigate complex degree requirements and identify optimal course sequences. These systems can alert students to potential scheduling conflicts, prerequisite issues, or opportunities to accelerate degree completion (Barret et al., 2019).
3.4 Student Information Portals and Administrative Systems
Student information portals serve as the primary digital interface between students and their institutions, providing access to course registration, financial aid information, grades, and various administrative services. AI technologies are increasingly being integrated into these portals to enhance user experience and provide more responsive support.
Chatbots have become a common feature of student information systems at U.S. universities. Georgia State University’s “Pounce” chatbot, developed in partnership with AdmitHub, provides real-time answers to student inquiries about admissions, financial aid, course registration, and other topics. The system uses natural language processing to understand student questions and provide relevant responses, available 24/7 including nights and weekends when human staff are not available. GSU reported that Pounce reduced summer melt by over 20% through text-based communication that helped students complete required tasks (Barret et al., 2019; Robinson, 2019).
Northern Virginia Community College (NVCC) implemented “Ace,” a chatbot designed to answer student questions and provide step-by-step guidance for processes like course registration. The system was particularly valuable for serving students outside traditional business hours, improving service quality while freeing staff time for more complex student needs. The Virginia Community College System (VCCS) has been a leader in adopting AI technologies to serve its diverse student population, which includes many working adults and first-generation college students (Barret et al., 2019).
The University of Southern California deployed “Tommy Bot” to handle basic student questions, while Colorado State University uses a chatbot for mental health support, demonstrating the versatility of conversational AI across different student service domains (Robinson, 2019).
Stanford University developed QuizBot, an AI chatbot that uses natural language interactions to help students review coursework. The system functions as an enhanced flashcard tool, providing supervised natural language processing-powered feedback. Stanford reported a 20% increase in student retention of course material through use of this AI-powered study tool (Rahman, 2024).
Kent State University implemented AI through the ALEKS (Assessment and Learning in Knowledge Spaces) system in its developmental math program. ALEKS provides placement testing, tracks student progress, and offers personalized learning recommendations based on each student’s demonstrated knowledge and learning patterns. This adaptive learning system adjusts the difficulty and sequencing of content based on student performance, providing a more individualized learning experience than traditional one-size-fits-all approaches (Rahman, 2024).
3.5 Additional AI Applications
Beyond the core areas of enrollment, onboarding, advising, and information systems, AI technologies are being applied across a wide range of functions in U.S. higher education. These include:
Research and Academic Integrity: Many U.S. institutions are grappling with how to address the use of generative AI tools like ChatGPT in student coursework and research. A case study at a large public U.S. university examined how CS1 (introductory computer science) students interact with generative AI tools when solving Python programming tasks. The research found that students use these tools for problem-solving, receiving explanations and examples, but also noted concerns about undesired help-seeking behaviors where students obtain complete solutions to homework assignments (Amoozadeh et al., 2024).
Student Engagement: Research at a U.S. university examined whether conversational AI, specifically ChatGPT, could support student engagement with learning content during in-class activities in large computer science classes. The study explored how AI tools might enhance active learning in settings where individual instructor attention is limited (Rani et al., 2025).
Student Evaluations and Feedback Analysis: A large public university developed a method for analyzing student evaluations of teaching (SETs) using natural language processing and large language models. The system can extract, embed, cluster, and summarize thousands of student comments to identify themes and provide actionable insights to instructors and administrators. This application demonstrates how AI can help institutions make sense of large volumes of qualitative feedback (Katz et al., 2024).
Financial Aid Communication: Research on chatbot implementation for student financial communication examined how enrollment management personnel and former students perceive AI-powered systems focused on financial aid questions. The study highlighted both the potential benefits of providing instant, accurate information about complex financial aid processes and the challenges of ensuring that automated systems can handle the nuances of individual student situations (Robinson, 2019).
