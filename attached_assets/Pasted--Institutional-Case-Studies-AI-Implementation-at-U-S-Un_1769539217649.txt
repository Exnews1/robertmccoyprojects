. Institutional Case Studies: AI Implementation at U.S. Universities
4.1 Georgia State University
Georgia State University has emerged as a national leader in AI implementation for student success. As a large, diverse public research university serving over 50,000 students in Atlanta, GSU has made strategic investments in AI technologies to improve outcomes for its student population, which includes high percentages of first-generation college students, Pell Grant recipients, and students from underrepresented minority groups.
GSU’s most notable AI initiative is its conversational AI system for reducing summer melt, described in detail in Section 3.2. The university implemented this system in 2016, using conversational technologies and AI to support students through pre-enrollment, enrollment, and orientation phases. The results were striking: AI-driven intervention increased enrollment by 3.3 percentage points and reduced failed summer matriculation by 21% (Riep et al., 2024).
Building on this success, GSU expanded its use of AI to include the Pounce chatbot for ongoing student support, AI-driven advising systems, and predictive analytics for identifying at-risk students. The university’s approach has been characterized by a focus on equity, using AI not to replace human advisors but to ensure that all students receive timely support regardless of when they need it or their familiarity with navigating university systems (Barret et al., 2019; Kohun et al., n.d.).
GSU’s AI implementations have been studied extensively by researchers and have influenced practices at other institutions. The university’s success demonstrates that AI can be particularly valuable at institutions serving diverse student populations where traditional support models may not reach all students effectively.
4.2 Stanford University and MIT
Stanford University and the Massachusetts Institute of Technology represent a different institutional context—highly selective, well-resourced research universities with strong technical capabilities. These institutions have approached AI implementation with emphasis on innovation, research, and pushing the boundaries of what AI can accomplish in educational settings.
Stanford developed QuizBot, an AI-powered study tool that provides personalized review support to students. The system uses natural language processing to engage students in interactive review sessions, adapting to their responses and providing targeted feedback. Stanford reported significant improvements in student retention of course material through this tool (Rahman, 2024).
Stanford has also been a leader in AI policy development and research on AI governance in higher education. The Stanford Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence (HAI) Institute produces influential research on AI policy, including the annual AI Index Report which tracks AI regulations and policy developments in the United States and globally. The 2024 AI Index Report documented a sharp increase in AI-related regulations in the U.S., with 25 AI-related regulations in 2023 compared to just one in 2016, representing a 56.3% increase in a single year (Stanford HAI, 2024).
MIT has been mentioned in the context of creating an “AI-centric college,” though specific details of implementation were limited in available sources. MIT’s approach reflects its institutional mission of technological innovation and its role in advancing the field of artificial intelligence itself (Robinson, 2019).
Harvard University has been referenced in research on AI applications for assessing programming assignments and in studies of AI policy development at U.S. universities. As part of the Ivy League and a leader in higher education policy, Harvard’s approaches to AI governance influence practices at other institutions (Mallik et al., 2023).
4.3 University of Maryland
The University of Maryland’s A. James Clark School of Engineering provides an important case study in developing AI systems with explicit attention to equity and bias mitigation. The school developed a bias-aware AI chatbot for engineering advising, specifically designed to address inequities experienced by underrepresented and first-generation students in engineering programs (Kartholy et al., 2025).
This implementation reflects growing recognition in U.S. higher education that AI systems must be designed with careful attention to potential biases. Engineering programs have historically struggled with diversity, and the University of Maryland’s approach demonstrates how AI can be part of equity-focused interventions rather than tools that perpetuate existing disparities.
The bias-aware chatbot was designed to complement traditional advising services, not replace them. This hybrid approach recognizes that while AI can provide efficient access to information and basic guidance, complex advising situations still require human judgment, cultural competence, and the ability to understand individual student contexts (Kartholy et al., 2025).
4.4 Community Colleges and Regional Institutions
Community colleges and regional universities serve the majority of U.S. higher education students, yet they often have fewer resources for technology implementation than flagship research universities. However, several community colleges have been leaders in AI adoption, recognizing that their student populations—often working adults, first-generation students, and those balancing multiple responsibilities—can particularly benefit from AI-powered support.
Northern Virginia Community College (NVCC) implemented the “Ace” chatbot to provide 24/7 support to students. As part of the Virginia Community College System (VCCS), NVCC serves a diverse student population across multiple campuses in the Washington, D.C. metropolitan area. The chatbot helps students navigate registration processes, find answers to common questions, and access resources outside traditional business hours (Barret et al., 2019).
Elon University, a private university in North Carolina, has implemented AI for course tracking and planning, helping students navigate degree requirements efficiently. Kent State University in Ohio has used the ALEKS adaptive learning system in developmental math, an area where many community college and regional university students need support (Rahman, 2024).
Research on AI policy in community college education has highlighted the unique challenges these institutions face. Community colleges operate within state systems with varying levels of autonomy, serve students with diverse needs and preparation levels, and must balance innovation with limited resources. The policy landscape for AI in community colleges reflects the broader federal structure of U.S. education, with different authorities at national, state, and institutional levels (Norton, 2024).
