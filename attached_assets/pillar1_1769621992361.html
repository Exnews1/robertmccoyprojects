Pillar 1: Military Learner Career Mobility

Evidence from 174 peer-reviewed sources (2015-2026)

Executive Summary

Post-9/11 veterans show mixed civilian employment and earnings outcomes: higher education uptake but variable job matches, persistent underemployment for some groups, and rising disability payments. Rigorous interventions and credential translation improve employment; policy should target skill alignment and benefit navigation.

Layer 2: Detailed Research Synthesis

Key Cross-Study Statistics
  • Enrollment and degree effects: Post-9/11 GI Bill recipients increased months enrolled and marginally raised bachelor's attainment for induced enrollees (+1.2 percentage points) while showing a small negative effect on average annual earnings nine years post-separation (≈$900) (Barr et al., 2021).
  • RCT employment gains: Intensive multifaceted employment coaching raised employment and earnings substantially: coached participants had higher employment rates and averaged materially greater income over one to two years (e.g., 95% vs 83% employment at two years and ≈$16,872 greater cumulative income) (Bond et al., 2023).
  • Unemployment comparisons: Post-9/11 veterans were reported more likely to be unemployed than comparable nonveterans in some CPS-based work (7.2% vs 6.0% cited in one study) (Ordway, 2020).
  • Underemployment prevalence: Self-reported underemployment driven by skill/credential mismatch was prevalent three years post-transition, with higher risk among enlisted, non-White, degree-holders, and those with PTSD symptoms (Davenport et al., 2020).
Methodological Strengths

Strengths include randomized designs that establish causality for employment interventions, large administrative datasets that capture long-run fiscal trends, and national survey supplements enabling population comparisons.

Barriers & MOS Transferability

Transition barriers cluster into credentialing and recognition, employer perception gaps, health-related constraints, and access/navigation obstacles.

Critical Finding: Where MOS tasks are highly military-specific or lack civilian credential analogues, veterans encounter downward mobility or underemployment.

Layer 2: Barrier Analysis

Credential Mismatch

Civilian employers often require licenses or civilian credentials that do not map directly to military MOS training, limiting direct hiring matches. This structural barrier affects veterans across multiple occupational categories.

Skill Translation Gaps

Veterans and employers report difficulty translating military tasks into civilian job descriptions and assessing competency signals; occupation clustering suggests better outcomes where overlap is clear.

Health & Disability Impacts

Increases in disability compensation and service-connected conditions complicate continuous employment and earnings trajectories for some cohorts, requiring integrated health and employment services.

Program Access Barriers

Veterans value barrier-reduction features (transportation, benefit navigation) but many programs do not offer the components veterans find most helpful; lower-rank veterans were less likely to use valued components.

MOS Transferability Specifics
  • Overrepresentation sectors: Veterans disproportionately enter occupations with technical overlap (maintenance, IT, protective services), and incomes in overlapping fields tend to be similar or modestly higher than nonveterans.
  • Transferability failure points: Where MOS tasks are highly military-specific or lack civilian credential analogues (e.g., certain weapons, tactical roles), veterans encounter downward mobility.
  • Employer-side barriers: Employers report uncertainty about how to evaluate military training, length of service, and rank as predictors of civilian job performance.

Outcomes by Education Type
  • Degree and earnings tradeoffs: While PGIB increased college participation and modestly boosted degree attainment for some, some groups (lower AFQT, less occupationally skilled) experienced worse longer-run earnings trajectories, attributed to lost labor market experience during extended schooling.
  • Sector effects on debt: Veterans from for-profit institutions frequently graduate with higher debt burdens and do not consistently obtain superior earnings relative to public institution graduates.
  • Vocational training benefits: Veterans who used the benefit for vocational or job-specific training often realized faster labor market attachment.
Policy Concerns

Qualitative and small-N studies point to aggressive recruitment by some proprietary institutions targeting GI Bill dollars, prompting recommendations for stronger oversight and veteran supports.

Theoretical Frameworks & Methods

Scholars apply human capital, signaling, occupational mobility, and transition frameworks to explain veterans' career mobility; rigorous methods vary from RCTs to quasi-experimental approaches.

Layer 2: Theoretical & Methodological Analysis

Dominant Theoretical Perspectives
  • Human capital: Military training and post-service education are framed as investments in human capital; transferability depends on specificity and market demand for acquired skills.
  • Signaling and credentialing: Veterans may require civilian credentials to signal competence; lack of recognized certifications reduces employer willingness to hire at commensurate levels.
  • Life course and transition theory: Models emphasize multiple life domains (health, financial, social) interacting with employment outcomes and adaptive processes over time.
  • Institutional frameworks: Program evaluations conceptualize career mobility as mediated by service access, barrier reduction components, and coaching intensity.
Methodological Approaches
  • RCTs: Provide causal evidence for intensive employment interventions; strengths include internal validity and clear treatment definitions (Bond et al., 2023).
  • Quasi-experimental: Long-run earnings and fiscal trends use administrative records and quasi-random assignment to infer causal links.
  • Longitudinal surveys: TVMI, Baccalaureate and Beyond permit multi-domain outcome tracking but can be limited by attrition and self-report bias.
  • Cross-sectional comparisons: CPS and ACS-based comparisons facilitate population contrasts but require careful adjustment for selection.
Critical Synthesis

Heterogeneity in methods explains divergent findings: RCTs show clear benefits for coached job-search, while observational studies of education benefits find mixed long-run earnings outcomes because of selection into schooling and differences in program types.

Measurement inconsistencies (definition of underemployment, short vs long follow-up) impede straightforward meta-analytic pooling, indicating a need for harmonized outcome metrics across studies.

Policy Implications & Recommendations

Priority Recommendations

  • Scale evidence-based employment programs with RCT validation
  • Implement credential translation and bridge programs
  • Target supports to high-risk subgroups (enlisted, non-White, PTSD)
  • Strengthen oversight of institutional recruitment
  • Integrate health and employment services

Research Priorities

  • Rigorous scale-up with evaluation
  • DOD-VA-ED data integration
  • Standardize outcome metrics
  • Long-term returns to vocational vs degree programs
